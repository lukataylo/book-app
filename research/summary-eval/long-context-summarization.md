# Long-Context Summarization: Fighting "Lost in the Middle" in a Chunk → Reduce Book-Summary Pipeline

A research memo synthesizing the long-context and long-document-summarization literature into concrete recommendations for this app's map-reduce transformation pipeline (`Chunker.swift`, `TransformationEngine.swift`). Compiled from peer-reviewed papers, official model-provider guidance, and reproducible benchmarks.

---

## Executive Summary

- **The problem is real and well-documented, not a hunch.** LLMs show a robust U-shaped recall curve over long contexts — best at the start and end, worst in the middle — first quantified by Liu et al. (2023/2024) and since replicated for retrieval, QA, *and* summarization specifically.[^1][^6]
- **Chunking does not automatically fix positional bias — it can just move it up a level.** BooookScore found Claude 2's hierarchical/incremental summaries still "omit key information present in the beginning or middle of the input… in favor of focusing on the end of the book."[^4] Positional bias reappears at the seam-merge and final-synthesis steps, not just inside a single 80K-token chunk.
- **Hierarchical merging (chunk-then-merge) is more coherent; incremental/refine retains more detail — pick per failure mode, not by default.** BooookScore: hierarchical scores higher on its coherence metric (89.1 vs. 82.5) but incremental is preferred for level of detail 83% of the time vs. 11% for hierarchical.[^4] This app's current pipeline (map + pairwise seam-rewrite + naive join, no real "reduce") gets neither strategy's safeguards.
- **Recursive merging amplifies hallucination unless context is carried forward explicitly.** Ou & Lapata (2025) show plain hierarchical merging lets errors compound across merge levels, and that explicitly propagating salient entities/context between merge stages measurably reduces this.[^9] This is the single most actionable architectural lever for `TransformationEngine`.
- **Density-forcing without a coverage checklist just concentrates on already-covered material.** Chain of Density shows that forcing entity density up via iterative "identify-what's-missing, then rewrite" passes measurably reduces lead bias and pulls in mid-document content — but only because each pass explicitly names missing entities first.[^5]
- **Positional instructions are a cheap, validated mitigation.** Anthropic's own long-context guidance says to put source documents before the instructions/query (query-at-end can improve response quality up to 30% in their tests) and to have the model quote relevant source passages before doing the real task — both reduce reliance on positional memory.[^8]
- **Recommendation for this codebase:** insert a per-chunk fact/claim extraction step before compression, build chapter/section-level intermediate summaries with an explicit "carry-forward" entity/fact ledger between merge steps (not just seam-smoothing prose), and add a final coverage-checklist cross-reference pass that verifies content from all three thirds of the book before accepting the output.
- **Recommendation for eval:** build a "positional recall" metric — extract N verifiable facts per third of the source book, then LLM-judge whether the final summary reflects each fact, and flag books where recall in the middle third falls significantly below the first/last thirds (see the eval section below for a concrete protocol).

## 1. Lost in the Middle (Liu et al., 2023/2024, TACL)

**Citation:** Nelson F. Liu, Kevin Lin, John Hewitt, Ashwin Paranjape, Michele Bevilacqua, Fabio Petroni, Percy Liang, "Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts," *Transactions of the Association for Computational Linguistics* (TACL) 12 (2024). arXiv preprint July 2023. https://aclanthology.org/2024.tacl-1.9/ — https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172 — code/data: https://github.com/nelson-liu/lost-in-the-middle

**Finding.** Testing multi-document QA and synthetic key-value retrieval across several models (including GPT-3.5-Turbo and Claude-1.3, plus open models), the authors varied *only* the position of the one relevant document/fact within a context of otherwise-irrelevant "distractor" documents, holding content and count fixed. Performance is consistently highest when the relevant item is at the very start (primacy) or very end (recency) of the context, and drops substantially — independent secondary sources converge on roughly 15–25 percentage points — when it sits in the middle, producing a U-shaped accuracy curve.[^1][^6] Notably, this happens even for models explicitly marketed with extended context windows, and in some multi-document settings adding more (irrelevant) context caused GPT-3.5-Turbo's accuracy to fall *below* its zero-context / closed-book baseline — i.e., more context was actively harmful once the needed fact was buried mid-context.[^1] *Note: we could not independently re-verify the paper's exact per-position percentage table from primary source text during this research pass; the qualitative U-shape and the "middle degrades 15–25pp, sometimes below closed-book" characterization are corroborated by multiple independent secondary sources, but exact numbers should be treated as approximate until checked against the original figures.*

**Mechanism.** The effect is attributed to how the models' attention/effective use of context is not uniform across position — models rely on positional heuristics learned from training data distributions (where salient information disproportionately appears at document starts/ends) rather than uniformly attending to all input positions. This is a property of the model's context utilization, not the content itself: swapping which document is "relevant" while holding position fixed reproduces the same curve.

**Architectural implication.** Any pipeline stage that concatenates multiple pieces of content into one LLM call (seam-rewriting, final synthesis, coverage-checklist cross-referencing) inherits this bias. It is not sufficient to have chunked the book — every *combination* step downstream of chunking is itself a "long context" call and is subject to the same U-shape. This directly motivates keeping combination steps small (few items at a time) and using position-aware prompting (see §6) rather than trusting a single large "merge everything" call.

## 2. Needle-in-a-Haystack Methodology (Kamradt et al.) and Its Application to Summarization

**Citation:** Greg Kamradt, "Needle In A Haystack — Pressure Testing LLMs" (2023, ongoing follow-ups), original methodology and results thread: https://x.com/GregKamradt/status/1727018183608193393 — reproductions/write-ups: https://github.com/gkamradt/LLMTest_NeedleInAHaystack — see also LangChain's multi-needle extension: https://www.langchain.com/blog/multi-needle-in-a-haystack

**Methodology.** A synthetic fact (the "needle," e.g., an out-of-place sentence) is inserted into filler text (the "haystack") at a controlled *depth* (0–100% through the document) and a controlled *context length*, then the model is asked to retrieve the needle. Repeating across a grid of (length × depth) and plotting pass-rate as a heatmap isolates positional recall from confounds like topical relevance, producing exactly the kind of clean signal Liu et al.'s controlled QA/retrieval experiments also produced — the middle-depth cells are reliably the weakest across most models tested, corroborating the U-shape.[^2]

**Applied to summarization specifically.** The canonical NIAH test is a *retrieval* task (needle is either present or absent, verbatim), and most published NIAH results are QA/retrieval, not summarization. However: (a) BooookScore explicitly frames its coherence-error analysis as a positional phenomenon on summarization output, finding Claude 2 systematically drops beginning/middle content in favor of the end of the book — effectively an unstructured, real-document version of a needle test applied to summarization;[^4] (b) "On Positional Bias of Faithfulness for Long-Form Summarization" (Wan et al., 2024) is a direct, purpose-built needle-style study *for* summarization — it perturbs the position of the "important" (gold, salient) source document within multi-document summarization inputs and measures whether the resulting summary faithfully reflects it, finding the same U-shape: faithfulness is lowest when the important document is placed in the middle.[^6] We were unable to verify an established, standardized "needle-in-a-haystack for summarization" benchmark with the same broad adoption as the QA-oriented NIAH suite — this appears to be an open area rather than a solved one, which is directly relevant to the eval section below.

**Architectural implication.** The NIAH grid-search methodology (length × depth) is directly reusable as an *evaluation harness pattern* for this app: instead of "can the model find sentence X," ask "does the final summary demonstrably reflect a known fact planted/known to exist at depth Y in the source." This is the basis for the eval methodology proposed later in this memo.

## 3. Recursively Summarizing Books with Human Feedback (Wu et al., 2021, OpenAI)

**Citation:** Jeff Wu, Long Ouyang, Daniel M. Ziegler, Nisan Stiennon, Ryan Lowe, Jan Leike, Paul Christiano, "Recursively Summarizing Books with Human Feedback," 2021. https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.10862

**Finding.** The paper's method — the direct ancestor of "map-reduce for books" — fine-tunes GPT-3 with human feedback (behavioral cloning + reward modeling) to summarize small sections of a book, then recursively summarizes those summaries (summary-of-summaries, several levels deep) until a single whole-book summary results. Its key claimed benefit is *evaluability*: human labelers can usefully grade the top-level output, or spot-check any single node in the recursion tree, without having read the entire underlying book themselves, because each node's inputs are small enough to verify directly. Reported outcomes: summaries were rated comparable to human-written summaries on a meaningful fraction of books, and the method achieved then-state-of-the-art results on BookSum.[^3]

We were **not able to verify, from the sources checked, a specific quantitative breakdown of information loss at each recursion depth or a length-dependent degradation curve** — the abstract and secondary summaries describe the architecture and aggregate human-preference results but do not give a per-level loss curve. This should be treated as an open question rather than a confirmed finding; if a precise per-level degradation number is needed, the full PDF's results tables should be checked directly. What *is* directly stated is the qualitative motivation: recursive decomposition trades some fidelity (each level is a lossy compression of the level below) for tractable, scalable human oversight — the same trade this app is making when it chunks a book and asks an LLM to reduce over the chunks.

**Architectural implication.** This paper establishes the core pattern this app already uses (map over chunks, then reduce) but is explicit that recursion depth is a lossy-compression pipeline: every additional level of "summarize the summaries" is another opportunity to drop mid-content, and there is no free lunch — the fix has to be architectural (tracking what survives each level), not just "add more levels."

## 4. BooookScore (Chang et al., 2024, ICLR)

**Citation:** Yapei Chang, Kyle Lo, Tanya Goyal, Mohit Iyyer, "BooookScore: A systematic exploration of book-length summarization in the era of LLMs," ICLR 2024 (Oral). https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.00785 (v4: https://arxiv.org/html/2310.00785v4) — code: https://github.com/lilakk/BooookScore

**Finding.** The first systematic human-annotated study (1,193 annotations over GPT-4-family summaries of 100 recent books) of coherence errors in LLM book-length summarization, comparing two dominant workflows: **hierarchical** (chunk-then-merge: summarize chunks independently, then merge summaries pairwise/tree-wise) vs. **incremental** (refine: maintain one running summary, updated chunk by chunk). They identify **eight recurring error types**: entity omission, event omission, causal omission, discontinuity (narrative flow breaks across time/perspective), salience errors (irrelevant detail included), language errors, inconsistency (plot/character contradictions), and duplication.[^4]

Head-to-head: **hierarchical merging produces more coherent output** (BooookScore metric: 89.1 vs. 82.5 for GPT-4) but **sacrifices detail** — human raters preferred incremental summaries for level of detail 83% of the time vs. 11% for hierarchical, while hierarchical won on structural coherence (59% vs. 35%). The authors attribute hierarchical's coherence advantage to its simpler per-step instructions (just "summarize" / "merge") versus incremental's harder per-step decision problem (what to keep, discard, and restructure given an already-written running summary). Critically for this memo: they explicitly document the lost-in-the-middle phenomenon in production summarization output — Claude 2 "omits key information present in the beginning or middle of the input... in favor of focusing on the end of the book."[^4]

**Architectural implication.** There is no strategy that's simply "better" — hierarchical trades detail for coherence, incremental trades coherence (and end-of-document lead bias, per Claude 2's behavior) for detail. This app's current pipeline (independent per-chunk transform + pairwise seam-rewriting + flat join) is closer to hierarchical/map-reduce but *lacks the actual merge/reduce step* that real hierarchical summarization has — seam-rewriting only touches the 800 characters on either side of each chunk boundary, it does not re-balance content across the whole book. This is a gap relative to both strategies in the literature: neither hierarchical merging nor incremental refinement is being fully implemented; the pipeline currently just concatenates independently-transformed chunks with local seam polish.

## 5. Chain of Density and Entity-Coverage Techniques

**Citation:** Griffin Adams, Alexander R. Fabbri, Faisal Ladhak, Eric Lehman, Noémie Elhadad, "From Sparse to Dense: GPT-4 Summarization with Chain of Density Prompting," Proceedings of the 4th New Frontier Summarization Workshop (EMNLP), 2023. https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.04269 — https://aclanthology.org/2023.newsum-1.7/

**Finding.** Chain of Density (CoD) is an iterative-densification prompt: GPT-4 first writes a deliberately entity-sparse summary (~70 tokens), then over 5 iterations repeatedly (a) identifies 1–3 salient entities from the source that are missing from the current summary, and (b) rewrites the summary at the *same length* to fuse in those entities — via abstraction/fusion/compression, not simple appending. Measured entity density rises from 0.089 (step 1) to 0.167 (step 5), eventually exceeding human-written summary density (0.151). Human preference peaked around step 3. Critically for this memo: **CoD summaries measurably reduce lead bias** — the average source-sentence rank of content pulled into the summary shifts from concentrated at the article's start toward broader coverage including the middle and end, as densification proceeds.[^5]

**Architectural implication.** The "identify what's missing, then rewrite to include it" loop is directly adaptable as a **coverage-checklist mechanism**: instead of (or in addition to) densifying, run a pass that explicitly asks "what salient entities/claims from [specific under-represented region of the source] are absent from the current draft?" and only then revise. This reframes coverage-checking as an explicit, auditable step rather than hoping a single "combine all chunk summaries" call implicitly balances content — which per §1 and §4 it will not do reliably on its own.

## 6. 2024–2026 Work on Long-Context Summarization Faithfulness and Mitigations

Several directly relevant, more recent works were found:

- **"On Positional Bias of Faithfulness for Long-Form Summarization"** (Wan et al., 2024). https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.23609 (HTML: https://arxiv.org/html/2410.23609v1). Introduces **LongFormFact**, a benchmark combining eight human-annotated long-form summarization datasets, and measures a U-shaped faithfulness curve directly on summarization (not just QA): summaries most faithfully represent source content from the beginning and end, and faithfulness to mid-document content is worst — with document-order perturbation showing up to ~7.8% swing in metric scores depending on where the "important" source document is placed, and faithfulness lowest when it's placed in the middle. Notably, they report that **explicit positional prompting** (telling the model to focus on specific parts of the document) improved targeted-position faithfulness across most datasets, while — in their specific experimental setup — hierarchical merging and incremental updating each *underperformed* simpler direct-generation baselines on overall faithfulness.[^6] *Caveat for this app: their "direct generation" baseline is only feasible when the whole document fits in one context call, which is not the case for full-length books at 80K-token chunks; the finding argues for keeping combination/merge steps as simple and few as possible, not for abandoning chunking, which remains a hard technical requirement here.*

- **"Hallucinate at the Last in Long Response Generation: A Case Study on Long Document Summarization"** (2025). https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.15291 (HTML: https://arxiv.org/html/2505.15291). A complementary, *output-side* positional bias: as generated summaries get longer, faithfulness degrades toward the *end of the generated summary* (not the source) — attributed to attention increasingly concentrating on the model's own already-generated tokens (nearly 3x more attention on the final generated sentence than early/middle ones, in the models studied) rather than the source, i.e., "generation drift" compounding late in a long output. They report chunk-then-merge approaches similar to BooookScore's hierarchical strategy largely avoid this because no single generation call is ever allowed to run very long — each chunk's output stays short, resetting the attention-drift clock.[^7] This is a distinct but related failure mode from source-side lost-in-the-middle and argues for keeping *individual generation calls* (map steps, merge steps, and the final synthesis step) short, not just keeping input chunks bounded.

- **"Context-Aware Hierarchical Merging for Long Document Summarization"** (Ou & Lapata, 2025, ACL Findings). https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.00977 — https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-acl.289/. Directly targets the failure mode this memo is about: plain hierarchical (chunk-then-merge) summarization can *amplify* hallucinations and factual drift across merge levels because each merge step only sees prior-level summaries, not the underlying source. Their fix — **carrying salient entities, key concepts, and structural context forward explicitly between merge levels**, rather than letting each merge step operate blind to what happened earlier in the tree — improves fidelity to mid-document content over standard hierarchical merging baselines.[^9] This is the most directly transferable mitigation for `TransformationEngine`'s reduce step.

- **Anthropic's official long-context prompting guidance** (Claude docs, "Long context prompting tips" / consolidated into the "Prompting best practices" guide). https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/docs/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/long-context-tips. Two concrete, directly applicable recommendations: (1) **place long-form source content near the top of the prompt, above instructions and the query** — queries placed at the end can improve response quality by up to 30% in Anthropic's own tests, especially for complex multi-document inputs; (2) for long-document tasks, **have the model quote the relevant source passages before doing the actual task** ("ground responses in quotes"), which helps it cut through positional noise rather than relying on implicit recall.[^8] Anthropic's broader "Effective context engineering for AI agents" post (Sept 2025) frames this as a general principle: context is a finite, degrading resource ("context rot") to be curated deliberately, not a bucket to fill and hope the model attends to uniformly.[^10]

**Architectural implication (combined).** Three independent, complementary levers emerge: (a) keep every LLM call — map, merge, and final synthesis — short, both on the input and the output side, since both source-side and generation-side attention drift scale with length; (b) do not let merge steps operate blind — carry forward an explicit ledger of entities/facts from earlier in the recursion tree, not just prose continuity; (c) use cheap, validated prompt-structure fixes (source-before-query ordering, quote-then-synthesize) at every stage that combines multiple pieces of already-summarized content, since these are the stages most exposed to positional bias per §1.

## 7. Map-Reduce vs. Refine (Sequential Update) — Practical Tradeoffs

**Sources:** Cross-referenced against BooookScore's hierarchical-vs-incremental framing[^4] and general practitioner writeups (LangChain summarization docs, Google Cloud's long-document summarization guide) — treated as corroborating engineering consensus rather than as a peer-reviewed primary source. https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/long-document-summarization-with-workflows-and-gemini-models

**Map-reduce** (independent per-chunk summaries, merged after): chunks are processed in parallel, so latency is roughly flat with respect to document length and the process is trivially resumable/cacheable per chunk — this is what `TransformationEngine.run` already does for the map phase, and it maps cleanly onto Claude's `cache_control: ephemeral` per-chunk caching already in use.[^11] Its weakness is exactly what this memo is about: independent chunk summaries have no visibility into each other, so the *merge* step has to do all the work of re-balancing coverage, and a naive merge (or, as in this app currently, no real merge at all — just a join plus local seam-smoothing) will inherit whatever the merge/combination call's own positional bias does to it.

**Refine / incremental** (maintain one running summary, updated chunk-by-chunk in document order): each step sees the full running summary plus the next chunk, so it *can* in principle maintain global coherence and cross-reference earlier content — but it is inherently sequential (no parallelism, latency scales with chunk count), and per BooookScore, in practice it tends to over-index on the most recently seen chunk, producing the lead-bias-inverted problem of *recency* bias toward the end of the book rather than *primacy* bias toward the start, unless carefully prompted to resist it.[^4]

**Which is more robust to mid-document loss, and why.** Neither is robust by default; the literature converges on: robustness comes from what happens *between* the independent/sequential steps, not from picking one topology over the other. Context-Aware Hierarchical Merging (§6) shows that a map-reduce/hierarchical topology becomes substantially more robust once merge steps carry forward explicit context rather than operating blind. Chain of Density (§5) shows an explicit "what's missing" check is what pulls a summarization process away from lead bias, independent of whether the underlying process is hierarchical or incremental. **Recommendation: keep the map-reduce topology this app already has (it fits the parallelism and caching architecture better), but make the reduce step do real work** — hierarchical merge with an explicit carried-forward fact ledger — rather than the current flat-join-plus-seam-rewrite, which structurally cannot correct for a chunk whose content got under-represented.

## Proposed Pipeline Sketch

This is a sequence of pipeline *stages*, not code — intended to slot into/replace parts of `TransformationEngine.run`'s current map (per-chunk transform) → seam-rewrite → join structure.

1. **Chunk as today** (`Chunker.swift`, chapter-boundary-aware, ~80K tokens + 4K overlap for cloud models). No change needed here — chunking quality is not the bottleneck the literature points to; the reduce side is.

2. **Per-chunk fact/claim extraction (new, before compression).** For each chunk, before generating the compressed/expanded prose output, run (or fold into the same call as) a structured extraction pass: named entities, key claims/arguments, plot events, and one identifying "location" tag (chapter/section number, and which third of the book by page position). This produces a small, structured, checkable artifact per chunk — directly modeled on Chain of Density's "identify what's salient" step[^5] and on Anthropic's "quote the source before synthesizing" guidance.[^8]

3. **Chapter/section-level intermediate summaries, carrying a fact ledger forward.** Group chunks by chapter (already a natural boundary per the chunker) and merge within small groups (2–4 chunks at a time, never “merge everything at once”), each merge call receiving: the prior chunks' prose outputs *and* the running fact ledger of entities/claims extracted so far — not just adjacent-chunk prose, as today's seam-rewrite does. This directly implements Context-Aware Hierarchical Merging's core fix (carry salient entities/context forward between merge levels, not just locally-adjacent text) instead of blind pairwise seam-smoothing.[^9]

4. **Explicit coverage-checklist cross-reference before final synthesis.** Before producing the whole-book summary, run a pass that takes the merged fact ledger (tagged by book-third) and the current draft, and asks: which extracted facts/claims/entities from each third are *not* reflected in the draft? This is CoD's "identify missing entities" step generalized from single-document density to whole-book positional coverage.[^5] Only facts flagged as missing from the middle third should trigger a revision pass — beginning/end coverage is, per the literature, the default the model will already skew toward, so checking specifically for middle-third gaps is the highest-leverage check.

5. **Final synthesis pass with position-structured prompting.** When producing the actual final summary text, follow Anthropic's long-context guidance directly: place the merged intermediate summaries/fact ledger before the instructions in the prompt, put the actual generation instruction at the end, and keep this call itself short in *output* length per §6's "hallucinate at the last" finding — if the target summary is long, this final pass should itself be chunked/section-by-section rather than one giant generation call.[^7][^8]

6. **Proportionality check as a hard gate, not just a soft goal.** After the final summary is produced, run the eval check described below (positional recall by book-third) as an automated gate before persisting the variant — not just as an offline eval metric. If middle-third recall falls below a threshold relative to first/last-third recall, trigger one targeted revision pass that's explicitly scoped to "add coverage of these specific missing middle-third facts without expanding overall length," mirroring CoD's fixed-length densification step.[^5]

Note on the existing seam-rewrite step: it should be kept (it demonstrably helps prose flow at chunk boundaries) but should not be mistaken for a reduce/merge step — it only ever sees 800 characters on either side of a boundary and has no visibility into whole-book coverage. Steps 3–4 above are what's currently structurally missing from `TransformationEngine`.

## Proposed Eval Methodology: Detecting "Forgot the Middle" Failures

**Goal:** a repeatable, at least partially automatable check for whether a generated book summary demonstrably under-represents the middle third of the source, independent of the summary's raw word-count allocation (a summary can "mention" the middle third in proportion to page count while still failing to convey its actual content — proportional word count is not the same as proportional *information* recall).

**Step 1 — Build a positional fact set per book (offline, one-time per eval book).**
Split the source text into thirds by character/token count (not by chapter, to avoid confounding with the chunker's own chapter-aware boundaries). From each third, extract N (e.g., 15–20) *verifiable, specific* facts/claims — named entities, concrete events, causal claims, direct quotes with attribution — using an LLM extraction pass with a prompt tuned for specificity (reuse the "concrete detail" bar from this repo's `ai-tells.md` — a fact should be something "a reader could look up," not a vague paraphrase). Human-spot-check a sample of the extracted facts for groundedness (they should be directly verifiable in the corresponding third, not hallucinated) — this makes the fact set itself a trusted eval fixture, reusable across every future summary-pipeline change without re-extracting each time.

**Step 2 — Positional recall scoring (per generated summary).**
For each of the 3N facts, an LLM-judge call determines whether the final summary "demonstrably reflects" the fact — not necessarily verbatim, but the specific entity/claim/event must be recoverable from the summary text. Score each fact binary (reflected / not reflected), or optionally 0/0.5/1 (absent / vaguely gestured at / clearly present) for finer resolution. Aggregate into three recall rates: `recall_first_third`, `recall_middle_third`, `recall_last_third`.

**Step 3 — The positional recall metric.**
Define a simple ratio: `positional_recall_gap = recall_middle_third / mean(recall_first_third, recall_last_third)`. A value near 1.0 indicates proportional coverage; a value well below 1.0 (e.g., under ~0.7, tunable threshold after establishing a baseline distribution across representative books) flags a "forgot the middle" failure. This is directly the summarization-specific analogue of the NIAH depth-heatmap methodology in §2 — instead of a single synthetic needle, use ~20 real, verifiable facts per third as a denser, content-grounded probe, and instead of binary present/absent on a single fact, aggregate into a rate.

**Step 4 — Making it automatable / CI-friendly.**
- Fact extraction (Step 1) and recall scoring (Step 2) are both single LLM calls with structured JSON output, which is straightforward to script as an offline eval harness (analogous in spirit to `eval-results.md`'s existing scoring conventions in this repo — worth aligning field names/format with that file rather than inventing a new schema).
- Run this against a fixed panel of representative books (varying length, genre, and chapter-marker structure, since the chunker's chapter-boundary detection behaves differently on structured vs. unstructured source text) on every pipeline change, not just ad hoc.
- Track `positional_recall_gap` as a first-class regression metric alongside whatever prose-quality/AI-tell metrics `eval-results.md` already tracks, so a change that improves fluency but silently worsens middle-third coverage gets caught.
- For the in-app "hard gate" version described in pipeline step 6 above, use a cheaper/faster variant: instead of a full 3N-fact panel, extract a smaller number of facts (e.g., 5 per third) at generation time itself and run the same recall check as a pre-persist gate, reserving the full N=15–20 panel for the offline regression-eval harness.

**Caveat.** We did not find an established, widely-adopted standard benchmark that already implements exactly this "positional recall for summarization" protocol — the closest published analogues are BooookScore's human-annotated omission-error taxonomy (§4) and LongFormFact's document-position perturbation study (§6), both of which measure closely related but not identical things (coherence errors, and faithfulness-by-source-document-position in multi-document news/sci summarization, respectively, rather than single-book positional recall of extracted facts). This protocol is a synthesis proposal built from those methodologies, not a reproduction of an existing published eval — it should be piloted on a small book panel and calibrated before being trusted as a hard release gate.
